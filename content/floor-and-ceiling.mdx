A control panel can only do what's drawn on it. That's obvious enough to skip
past, so here it is the other way round: everything a panel can do is visible,
and everything it can't do is invisible in exactly the same way — as nothing.
Four sliders are the whole language. There is no fifth thing you could type.

A text box makes the opposite trade. Nothing on it says what it can do, which
is the same as saying nothing on it says what it can't.

I've started thinking about this as a floor and a ceiling. The ceiling is what
an interface can express. The floor is how much you can get done without
already knowing something. A panel's floor and ceiling are the same height —
you can see the top from where you're standing, and there is nothing above it.
A text box has a very high ceiling and no floor at all.

The panel is not a strawman. Every dimension that square has is on it, so
there's no state the box can reach that the panel can't. And the thing behind
the box is a page of regular expressions, not a model — worth saying plainly,
because a demo that implies intelligence it hasn't built is arguing for
something other than what's on screen.

## what the extra ceiling actually buys

Type `heavier`. Three controls move at once — darker, bigger, sharper corners —
and the interesting part isn't that it beat dragging three sliders. It's that
nobody had to decide "heavier" was a thing.

Every control on a panel is a decision made in advance: this is worth exposing,
this isn't, this gets a slider and this gets buried two menus down. That list is
finite, and it's finalised before anyone touches the product. A text box doesn't
have the list. It never had to be right about what people would want, because it
never had to guess.

That's the freedom, and it's narrower than it sounds. The box can't do more —
same square, same four dimensions. It just wasn't designed around a prediction
of what you'd ask for.

## and what it costs

Ask it for something it doesn't know. `flip it`. `add a shadow`. `make it look
like a stop sign`.

Nothing happens. No error worth the name, no list of what would have worked, no
edge you can feel until you're already through it. The panel has no equivalent
failure — you cannot ask a slider for something it doesn't have. You can only
find out a text box's limits by hitting them, one guess at a time, and each
miss teaches you almost nothing about where the next one is.

There's a smaller version of the same problem in the word `lighter`. Lighter in
colour, or lighter in weight? My parser picks colour. A model would also pick
one, and be more gracious about it, and you would still have no way of knowing
which reading you were going to get before you pressed enter.

## everyone already knows this

Look at the chips under the box. I put them there and then spent a paragraph
arguing against them, which is roughly what the whole industry has done.

Every chat product ships some version: suggestion cards, prompt starters, slash
commands, a little toolbar for the four things people actually do. Those aren't
features. They're a control panel bolted back on next to the box, because the
box turned out to have no floor and somebody had to give people a way in.

## so which one

The bet a text box makes is that the ceiling matters more than the floor.

That's the right bet when the space of things people might want is genuinely
too large to enumerate — writing, code, images, anything where the request
worth serving is one nobody anticipated. It's the wrong bet when the space is
small and known. Nobody wants to type "turn the volume down a bit." The panel
wins every time the list is short enough to draw.

What I don't think is that the box is the lazy option. It's half an interface.
The other half is whatever gives it a floor without capping its ceiling, and
that's a design problem rather than a model problem — a better model raises the
ceiling and does nothing at all about the fact that you're sitting in front of
a blank box with no idea what to say to it.

Every fix so far has been a panel in disguise. I don't think the answer is a
bigger one. I think it's that the box should be able to say what it can do at
the moment you need to know, which is not the same as printing a menu. That's
the part I want to build next.
