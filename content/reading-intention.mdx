Every chat box has the same shape. One field, one button, and no idea what's
about to happen. You type, you send, and the first thing you learn about how
you were understood is whatever comes back.

Compare that to asking a person. They tell you what they heard — "so you want
me to rip out the old one?" — and you get to say *no, wait* before anyone's
done anything. That check costs a second and saves an afternoon.

We dropped it. Not for a reason: it just never made it into the pattern, and
now every product copies the same empty box.

So: what if it told you what it heard?

## What changes

Notice it's not showing you a number. It's changing what it *says*.

At high confidence it commits — "I'm about to fix." Drop below the line and the
same sentence goes soft — "I **might** be about to fix" — and it names the one
thing that would settle it. The percentage is the least useful thing on screen;
a bar that moves while the words stay certain is just decoration.

The case I care about most is hedging. Type *maybe*, *sort of*, *or something*,
and it hedges back. It knows the verb perfectly well — "add a dark mode" is
unambiguously a build — but knowing the verb isn't knowing the ask. Confidence
gets capped rather than nudged, because a clear verb inside a vague request
should never come back sounding sure.

That's the failure mode worth naming. Ask an assistant something woolly today
and you get a crisp, confident answer to a question you didn't quite ask. Your
uncertainty goes in and someone else's certainty comes out. The hedge gets
laundered, and you only find out downstream.

## How it actually works

The version above is keyword matching. Roughly forty words voting for four
verbs, a margin check between the top two, a length penalty, and a hedge list.
No model, a few dozen lines, and it runs as you type.

A real one wouldn't work like that. You'd get the reading from the same
model that's about to do the work — a cheap structured call returning a verb, a
one-line restatement, a confidence, and the missing piece, before the expensive
call runs. Model-based classification also catches what keywords can't: "this
worked yesterday" is a bug report with no bug words in it.

What wouldn't change is the interface. Same four things on screen, same rule
that low confidence rewrites the sentence instead of just dimming a bar. That's
the part I think is right, and it's why the demo is deliberately dumb — swapping
the classifier is an afternoon, and it wouldn't teach me anything about whether
the pattern works.

## The part I'm unsure about

There's an obvious objection: people will learn to ignore it. We're very good
at not seeing things next to the button we're trying to press, and a reading
that's usually right is a reading you stop reading.

I think that's survivable if it only speaks up when it's unsure — silence at
90%, a sentence at 40%. But that's a guess, and it's the kind of guess that
gets settled by watching ten people use it, not by arguing about it.

The narrow claim I'll defend: **show the reading while it's still cheap to
correct.** A system that says it's 40% sure before it spends two minutes being
wrong beats one that's confidently wrong faster.
