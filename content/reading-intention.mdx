Every chat interface has the same gap in it. You type something, you press
enter, and then you find out whether you were understood — by reading whatever
it produced. If it misread you, you've already paid for the answer.

The correction is cheap before you commit and expensive after. So why is the
understanding only ever shown afterwards?

## What it's doing

The reading is deliberately dumb — keyword matching, no model behind it —
because the thing I want to test is the interaction, not the inference. It
picks a verb, states what it's about to do, and shows how sure it is.

Confidence moves on three things. It drops when a second intent scores nearly
as well as the first, because that's genuine ambiguity rather than noise. It
drops on very short input, since three words rarely pin down a task. And it
drops when you hedge — *maybe*, *sort of*, *or something* — which is the part I
find most interesting.

Try "maybe add a dark mode or something" and watch it hedge back at you. You
were uncertain, so it's uncertain, and it says so. Most assistants launder your
hedge into a confident answer, which is exactly backwards.

## The bit that matters

Low confidence changes the *sentence*, not just the number. "I'm about to fix"
becomes "I might be about to fix", and it names the one thing that would
resolve it — how to reproduce, where it should live. A percentage alone is
decoration; a percentage that changes what the system says about itself is a
design decision.

I don't think this exact widget is the answer. The move I'm fairly convinced by
is narrower: **show the reading while it's still cheap to correct.** A model
that tells you it's 40% sure before it spends two minutes being wrong is worth
more than one that's confidently wrong faster.

Whether people would actually read it, or just press enter through it the way
they press enter through everything else, I genuinely don't know. That's the
part I'd want to test with real users rather than argue about.
