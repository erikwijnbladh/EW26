Most frontends work by inference. Something arrives, you look at the shape of
it, and you guess what it means. Is there a `campaigns` array? Is it non-empty?
Then draw an expander.

That works. It keeps working right up until it doesn't, and the reason it's a
bad deal isn't that it breaks — it's that a wrong guess and a right one look
identical from the inside. Nothing tells you which one you're doing.

## what inference actually costs

- **every new case is your problem.** the producer adds a variant, and the
  consumer needs a release to understand it.
- **the rule lives nowhere.** it's spread across conditionals, and no single
  place says what the data means.
- **you can't verify it.** correct output and lucky output are the same output.

## the alternative is boring

Let the producer say what it means, and render the declaration.

At BrightBid I built Audit, which pulls issues out of a Google Ads account and
puts them in one table. The trouble is that accounts aren't alike: different
accounts have different problems at different depths, so what the backend can
say about one is structurally unlike what it can say about another. Some rows
need to expand into campaigns underneath them. Some are just a row.

So the backend didn't hand over data for us to interpret. It attached
classifiers to the object headers, and the frontend rendered off those.
`has_nested_rows` and friends — ugly names, doing real work. The absence of one
was a signal too: nothing on the header meant render the following items
normally.

The frontend never looked at the payload to decide anything. It looked at what
the payload said about itself.

The formal name for this is a discriminated union, and it's older than I am.
The part worth restating is who does the work: **whoever knows the answer
should say it, rather than leaving it to be reconstructed by someone who
doesn't.**

## what it bought, and what it cost

Backend could ship an entire new issue type and the frontend rendered it
without a change. That held as long as both sides kept to the vocabulary, which
is the whole catch — the contract isn't in the type system, it's in the
agreement.

And when a classifier was missing, the row didn't render. Not an error, not a
fallback. It just wasn't there. That's the bill for refusing to guess: no
inference means no recovery either, and silence is a bad failure mode.

The fight wasn't with the backend engineers, who got it immediately. It was
upward, where the reasonable-sounding question is *why is it hard for frontend
to just do X for A, B and C sometimes.* From outside, "just handle it" is free.
The cost is invisible, deferred, and paid by whoever is holding the conditionals
next year.

For the record: the product had no users. I'm not claiming an outcome I don't
have. What I'm claiming is narrower — that when the producer changed, nothing
downstream had to.

## the same problem, unsolved

I keep running into the un-fixed version of this in model output.

A language model hands you a wall of text and you infer your way through it.
Which parts are solid. Which are invented. Which need checking before you paste
them anywhere. You're doing shape inspection on prose, and it has the same
property that made it a bad deal in a table: **a confident wrong sentence is
shaped exactly like a confident right one.**

We fixed this for APIs by making producers declare. Nothing in these interfaces
declares anything. There's no `has_nested_rows` for "this part I actually know"
versus "this part I assembled because it fit."

I don't think that's unsolvable. I think it's unbuilt, and I notice that almost
all the effort is going into making the output better rather than making it
legible. Better output raises the cost of the guess. It doesn't remove it.

Someone always pays for inference. Usually it's whoever is furthest from
knowing the answer.
