Here's something I believe but haven't fully thought through: writing the
code was never really a developer's job, and hand-drawing squares in Figma
was never really a designer's job. Those were just the most visible parts —
the most basic description of what we do.

Nobody hired a designer because they needed rectangles arranged. They hired
someone to decide which rectangles, why there, what to leave out, when it's
done. Same with code: two people with the same tools and the same brief ship
very different things, and the difference was never typing.

I think about this a lot now that AI does the visible part. It draws the
squares, it writes the code, and it's getting good at both. If you take the
narrowest reading of the roles, that looks like the end of them. I don't
read it that way. I think there's always room for designers and developers —
for developers, and maybe even more so for designers — we just have to look
past the most basic description of what our roles were.

What's left when the production step is cheap is everything that was
actually hard: knowing what to build, what good feels like, what to say no
to. I suspect that was always the job. The tools just made it easy to
confuse the artifact with the work.
