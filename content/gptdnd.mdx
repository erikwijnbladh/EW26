I don't know how to play D&D.

I've built and rebuilt this thing more times than I can count — it generates
whole campaigns now, sessions and encounters and stat blocks and maps — and I
still can't tell you whether any of it is good. I prompt it, I read what comes
back, and I go: okay, sure.

For a while that felt like the embarrassing part. I think it's actually the
interesting part.

## Not knowing changed what I built

Making things used to be expensive, and that expense quietly kept you inside
subjects you understood. Nobody spends six months on something they can't
judge. That's not true any more, and I'm a fairly clean example of it — I've
produced a lot of finished-looking material in a game I've never played.

So I couldn't rely on reading the output. I had to make the system tell me
whether it had done a good job:

- encounters print their arithmetic — thresholds, the multiplier for group
  size, measured difficulty against target — into the book rather than keeping
  it internal
- stat blocks get normalised, with outlier detection on the numbers that
  actually break a fight
- a mystery whose conclusion rests on fewer than three clues is reported as a
  defect
- safety limits are compiled into every prompt *and* re-checked against the
  finished text afterwards
- every run ends with a report I can read without knowing the game

That's what I ended up doing instead of having taste in the domain.

## The bit I think generalises

"This encounter is hard" is a sentence a model will write whether or not it's
true. "4,200 XP against a 3,800 threshold, ×1.5 for four creatures" is
something I can check, or argue with, or hand to someone who does know the
game.

I keep running into versions of this. At BrightBid I spent a lot of time
turning ad-bidding decisions into scores and reasons a customer could push
back on, which is the same shape of problem — a system making calls you can't
see inside, and an interface that has to make them arguable rather than just
confident.

I hadn't connected the two until I'd built this one.
