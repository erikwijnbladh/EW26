Two things about this project that I should say before anything else.

I don't know how to play D&D. And I didn't design the system — I ran a deep
research prompt, told it to build the thing, and shipped what came back. The
canonical graph, the quality gates, the encounter maths: I chose to keep them,
I didn't invent them.

I've rebuilt it more times than I can count and I still can't tell you if the
output is good. I prompt it, I read what comes back, and I go: okay, sure.

## That's the actual subject

I keep wanting to write this up as engineering, and every time I do it comes
out dishonest, because the interesting thing here isn't the pipeline. It's that
I produced eleven thousand lines of working software for a game I've never
played, in a domain where I can't evaluate a single decision, and I only
noticed afterwards that this was strange.

Making things used to be expensive, and that expense quietly kept people inside
subjects they understood. Nobody spent six months on something they couldn't
judge. That's gone now, and I'm a clean example of what replaces it — plausible,
finished-looking output, produced fast, by someone with no way to check it.

## The thing I'd defend

What I did do was decide the output had to prove itself rather than assert
itself. Encounters print their arithmetic instead of hiding it. A mystery whose
conclusion rests on fewer than three clues is reported as a defect. Safety
limits get re-checked against the finished text rather than just asked for in
the prompt. Every run ends with a report a non-player can read.

"This encounter is hard" is a sentence a model writes whether or not it's true.
"4,200 XP against a 3,800 threshold" is something someone who does know the
game can disagree with.

I keep running into that shape. At BrightBid it was turning bidding decisions
into scores a customer could argue with. Same problem, different domain: a
system making calls you can't see inside, and an interface whose job is to make
them checkable rather than convincing.
