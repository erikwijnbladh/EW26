I don't know how to play D&D.

That's the interesting part, and for a while I thought it was the embarrassing
part. I built a system that generates complete campaigns — sessions, encounters,
stat blocks, maps, handouts — and I am not qualified to tell you whether any of
it is good. I prompt it, I read the output, and I go: okay, sure.

## The new problem is evaluation, not generation

Making things used to be the expensive step, and that expense did quiet work
for us: it kept you inside domains you understood, because nobody spends six
months on something they can't judge. Generation is cheap now, and the guardrail
went with it. You can produce a finished-looking artefact in a field you know
nothing about, in an afternoon.

Which leaves you holding something confident and plausible with no way to tell
if it's right. That isn't a D&D problem. It's the problem with every interface
sitting on top of a model.

## If you can't judge it, build the judge

The only answer I found was to stop relying on my reading of the output and
make the system prove itself instead. So a run doesn't just produce a campaign,
it produces evidence:

- encounters carry their arithmetic — thresholds, the multiplier ladder,
  measured against target difficulty — printed into the book, not hidden
- stat blocks are normalised, with outlier detection on the numbers that
  actually break a fight
- a mystery whose conclusion rests on fewer than three clues is reported as a
  defect
- safety limits are compiled into every prompt *and* re-scanned against the
  finished text, because asking politely isn't a guarantee
- every run ends with a quality report I can read without knowing the game

None of that exists because it's rigorous engineering. It exists because I have
no taste here, and encoded judgement is what you use when you don't have the
real thing.

## Show the arithmetic, not the reasoning

The habit I'd keep from this: an opaque system earns trust by being auditable,
not by being articulate.

"This encounter is hard" is a claim, and a fluent model will produce it whether
or not it's true. "4,200 XP against a 3,800 threshold, ×1.5 for four creatures"
is something you can disagree with. The second one respects you; the first one
just sounds confident.

That's the same thing I was doing at BrightBid, turning ad-bidding decisions
into scores someone could argue with. I didn't notice it was the same problem
until I'd built this.
